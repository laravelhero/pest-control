Termites are often called "silent destroyers," and the nickname is well earned. They can feed on the wooden structure of a home for months or even years before a homeowner notices anything is wrong. By then, the damage — and the repair bill — can be severe. Termites cause billions of dollars in property damage every year, and most of it isn't covered by standard homeowner's insurance.

The best defense is early detection. Here are the warning signs every homeowner should know, and the habits that keep your biggest investment protected.

## Why termites are so dangerous

Unlike most pests, termites attack the very thing that holds your house up: the wood framing. They eat cellulose from the inside out, so a beam can look perfectly solid on the surface while being hollowed out within. Because the colony works out of sight, the structural impact accumulates quietly until it becomes a major — and expensive — problem.

## 1. Mud tubes

Subterranean termites, the most common and destructive type, build pencil-width "mud tubes" to travel between their underground colony and their food source while staying protected from open air. You'll often find these earthen tubes running up foundation walls, along crawl spaces, or near piers and pipes. If you see them, break a small section open; active termites will work to repair it within days.

## 2. Hollow or blistering wood

Tap on wood you suspect is affected. If it sounds hollow or papery, termites may have eaten the interior. You might also notice wood that looks blistered, sagging, or darkened, almost as if it has water damage. Floors that feel spongy and baseboards that crumble under light pressure are red flags worth investigating immediately.

## 3. Discarded wings and swarmers

In spring, mature colonies release winged termites called "swarmers" to start new colonies. After they fly, they shed their wings, leaving small piles of identical, translucent wings near windowsills, doors, and light sources. Finding these wings indoors is one of the strongest signs that a colony is active in or very near your home.

## 4. Frass (termite droppings)

Drywood termites push their droppings, called frass, out of their tunnels. Frass looks like tiny, ridged pellets, often resembling sawdust or coffee grounds, and tends to collect in small mounds beneath infested wood. If you sweep up a pile only to find it reappears, termites are likely the source.

## 5. Tight-fitting doors and windows

As termites tunnel through frames and the moisture from their activity warps wood, doors and windows can begin to stick or jam. While humidity can cause the same symptom, sudden stiffness combined with any other sign on this list deserves a closer look.

## How often should you inspect?

Prevention beats repair every time. We recommend:

- A professional termite inspection at least once a year.
- More frequent checks if you live in a warm, humid region or have had termites before.
- Quick action any time you spot mud tubes, wings, or frass.

Between professional visits, you can reduce your risk by keeping firewood and mulch away from the foundation, fixing leaks and drainage problems, and making sure wooden parts of the home don't contact the soil directly.

Don't disturb the area more than necessary, and don't rely on store-bought sprays — they may scatter the colony without eliminating it, making professional treatment harder.
